Watch a globular cluster grow old

A globular cluster is a ball of hundreds of thousands of stars, all born at the same moment from one giant cloud. Press Play and watch 13 billion years go by. Newborn stars first slide onto the main sequence; later the heaviest stars run out of fuel and peel off it. The place where stars are leaving, the turn-off point, creeps down the main sequence like the hand of a clock.

What you are watching

  1. Birth (first few million years). All the stars appear at once as cool, puffy protostars on the right. They shrink and slide left and down onto the main sequence. Heavy stars get there in thousands of years; small ones take hundreds of millions.
  2. Main sequence. Each star settles at a spot set by its mass: heavy ones hot and bright (top left), light ones cool and dim (bottom right).
  3. Peeling off. The heaviest stars run out of hydrogen first and swing up to the right as giants. As time passes, lighter and lighter stars follow.
  4. Death. Heavy stars explode as supernovae and vanish. Sun-like stars become giants, then shed their outer layers and fade into white dwarfs at the bottom left.

Why the turn-off point is a clock

All the stars are the same age, so the ones leaving the main sequence right now are exactly the ones whose lifetime equals the cluster’s age. Heavy stars live short lives; light stars live long ones. So the lower the turn-off, the older the cluster.

Globular clusters

About 150 globular clusters orbit our Milky Way. Each holds 100,000 to a million stars packed into a ball about 100 light-years across. Their turn-off points sit near Sun-like stars, which tells us they are 12 to 13 billion years old, among the oldest things in the galaxy. The famous one in the northern sky is M13 in Hercules.

Their old stars contain very little metal, so the helium-burning giants spread out in a horizontal row on the H-R diagram: the horizontal branch.
