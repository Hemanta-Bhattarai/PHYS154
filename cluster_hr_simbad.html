0Get the data from SIMBAD

Build your query, run it on SIMBAD in a new tab, and save the result as a .txt file with a clear name.

What SIMBAD is

SIMBAD, run by the Strasbourg astronomical data centre (CDS), is a database of published measurements for millions of objects. A criteria query asks for every object matching a rule. Ours is: stars (maintypes = "*") inside a circle around the cluster centre that have both a B and a V magnitude.

A · Build your query

  1. Type your ID: your first name followed by your birth month and day as four digits (MMDD). Example: Manu, born December 22, types manu1222.
  2. Choose 1st, 2nd or 3rd assigned cluster. Your instructor has given every student their own three clusters, picked from a list of 100 using your ID, so type it exactly the same way every time. The centre and radius fill in automatically from published catalogue values.
  3. Press Copy query and paste it into SIMBAD (part B). The text in the gold box is exactly what SIMBAD needs.
  4. Optional: find the same cluster in Stellarium (equatorial grid on, J2000) to see where it is on the sky. The class practice clusters M45, M44 and M67 are still in the list.

B · Run it on SIMBAD (opens in a new tab)

  1. Open SIMBAD in a new tab ↗. This page stays open here so you can come back. Under Queries, choose by criteria.
  2. Click Output options. In the list display column select only: Identifier, Object type, Coordinates 1, Proper motion, Parallax, Radial velocity, Fluxes (tick U, B, V, R, I), Spectral type. Uncheck everything else, then Save.
  3. Go back to the criteria query page. Choose the display radio button on the right.
  4. Click in the big query box and paste your query (Ctrl+V, or ⌘+V on Mac).
  5. Click submit query and wait until the table appears; it can take a little while.
  6. Scroll to the bottom and click ASCII. You should see a plain-text table with columns separated by |.

C · Save it as a .txt file

Name your file:  manu1222_C1-NGC2264_SIMBAD_2026-10-28.txt
Your ID, the cluster, the word SIMBAD and the date, joined by underscores, no spaces. Then you, your instructor and the tool can all tell files apart.
  1. On the ASCII page press Ctrl+S (⌘+S on Mac).
  2. Type the file name above. If there is a “Save as type” or “Format” menu, choose Text or Plain text, not “Webpage, Complete”. Make sure the name ends in .txt.
  3. Save it somewhere you will find it (Downloads or your lab folder).
If saving doesn’t give you a .txt, use either backup:
  • On the ASCII page press Ctrl+A then Ctrl+C, paste into the text box in step 1 below and press Save pasted text as .txt. It downloads with the right name.
  • Or paste into Notepad (Windows) or TextEdit (Mac: Format → Make Plain Text first) and save with the name above.

If SIMBAD misbehaves

  • Only “Number of objects” shows: you didn’t pick the display radio button. Go back and select it.
  • Columns are missing or you get the same table after changing output options: SIMBAD reuses its answer when the query text is identical. In SIMBAD’s query box, change the RA seconds by 0.1 (for example 58.0s to 58.1s) and resubmit; that tiny shift doesn’t change which stars you get.
  • No radvel or Mag B / Mag V columns: redo the output options (step B2).

2Choose an RV window and trim

Keep only stars whose radial velocity (RV) is between RVmin and RVmax.

Why radial velocity picks out the cluster

Stars in a cluster were born together from one gas cloud, so they still move through space together. Their radial velocities (speed toward or away from us, measured from the Doppler shift of their spectral lines) are almost the same, typically within a few km/s. Field stars along the same line of sight are unrelated and have velocities spread over tens of km/s. In a histogram of RV, the cluster shows up as a tall, narrow spike sitting on a low, wide spread of field stars.

Stars with no RV measurement can’t be tested, so trimming removes them too, just like deleting the ~ rows in the spreadsheet.

Do this

  1. Find the tall spike in the histogram. That is the cluster.
  2. Drag across the histogram from the left edge of the spike to its right edge. The shaded band shows your window and the boxes fill in RVmin and RVmax. You can also type the numbers.
  3. Include the whole spike and its shoulders; don’t be too picky. Bars inside the window turn gold.
  4. Press Trim data. Everything outside the window, and every star without an RV, is removed.
Not sure? uses median ± 3σ, where σ comes from the median absolute deviation so stray field stars don’t inflate it.

3Compute B−V and the apparent magnitude

Add two new columns, then plot them against each other.

What B−V measures

Mag B is a star’s brightness through a blue filter and Mag V through a yellow-green (visual) filter. Their difference is the colour index:
B − V = mB − mV = −2.5 log10(FB/FV) + constant
Hot stars put out relatively more blue light, so they have small or negative B−V (about −0.3 for a 30 000 K star). Cool stars are red and have large B−V (about +1.5 near 3 500 K). The Sun has B−V ≈ 0.65. So B−V is a thermometer: it plays the role of temperature on the HR diagram.

Why apparent magnitude is good enough

All cluster stars are at essentially the same distance, so a star that looks brighter really is more luminous. Apparent magnitude V therefore stands in for luminosity, shifted by the same distance offset for every star. Remember magnitudes run backwards: smaller numbers are brighter, so the vertical axis is flipped to put the bright stars at the top.

Do this

When you press Proceed, the page will, for every star you kept:
  1. make a new column B−V = Mag B − Mag V;
  2. make a column App Mag = Mag V;
  3. draw a scatter plot with B−V across (x) and App Mag up (y, reversed).

4Find the main sequence and the turnoff

Draw the main-sequence line, mark the turnoff point, and use it to judge the cluster’s age.

The main sequence

For most of its life a star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. During that stage its temperature and luminosity are set mainly by its mass, so stars of different mass fall along one diagonal band running from hot, bright stars (upper left) to cool, faint stars (lower right). That band is the main sequence. Massive stars are far more luminous, roughly L ∝ M3.5, so they burn their fuel much faster:
tMS ≈ 10 Gyr × (M / M☉)−2.5
A 1 M☉ star lasts about 10 Gyr; a 5 M☉ star only about 0.2 Gyr (this scaling is rough).

The turnoff point and what it means

All stars in a cluster formed at about the same time. As the cluster ages, the most massive stars run out of core hydrogen first, swell, cool and leave the main sequence, moving up and to the right toward the giants. The top of the main sequence therefore gets “eaten away” from the bright blue end. The turnoff point is where the stars bend away from the main-sequence line.

The stars at the turnoff are just now finishing their main-sequence life, so the cluster’s age equals the main-sequence lifetime of turnoff stars. A turnoff high and blue means only very massive stars have died yet: a young cluster. A turnoff low and yellow means even stars not much heavier than the Sun are gone: an old cluster.

Do this

  1. Optionally tidy the x-axis with the B−V limits below the plot to leave out a few stray points.
  2. Press Draw main-sequence line, then click two points along the middle of the lower, fainter part of the diagonal band, one near the faint red end and one higher up where the band is still straight. A straight line is drawn through them across the plot. Stars in the lower part of the band are unevolved, so they define the line best.
  3. Follow the band upward toward the bright blue end. Press Mark turnoff and click where the stars stop following your line and bend up or to the right. If the band just runs out at the top, mark its brightest, bluest well-populated point.
  4. Ignore the occasional lone star above the turnoff on the blue side (a “blue straggler”, usually a merged binary) and the stars up and to the right (giants that have already left the main sequence).
  5. Read the age estimate under the plot, then write your own sentence in step 5.
