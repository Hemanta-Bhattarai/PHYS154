How old is the Crab Nebula?

In 1054, Chinese astronomers saw a “guest star” bright enough to see in daylight. Today, at that spot, sits a glowing cloud of debris that is still flying apart. If you measure how far its filaments moved between 1956 and 1999, you can run the movie backward and find the year it all started.

Step 0 · the physicsRunning the movie backward

When the star exploded, its debris flew outward from the center in every direction. Faster pieces went farther; slower pieces stayed closer. If nothing has sped them up or slowed them down, then every piece obeys the same simple rule:

distance = speed × (time since explosion)

That means each filament’s distance from the pulsar (the dead star left at the center) grows in proportion to time. Measure a filament’s distance in 1956 and again in 1999, and you know how fast it is moving. Divide its 1956 distance by that speed, and you get how long it has been traveling.

The handout breaks this into four arithmetic steps for each feature:

  1. Movement: Δd = d1999 − d1956 (mm)
  2. Speed: v = Δd ÷ 43 yr (mm/yr)
  3. Travel time: t = d1956 ÷ v (yr)
  4. Explosion year: T = 1956 − t

The same thing in one line

Free (homologous) expansion from a point at time T: every parcel has r(t) = v(t − T). Writing this at t1 = 1956 and t2 = 1999 and dividing,

r2/r1 = (t2 − T)/(t1 − T)  ⇒  T = t1 − (t2 − t1) · r1/(r2 − r1)

Three consequences worth telling students:

  • Units cancel. Only the ratio r2/r1 matters, so millimeters on a printout, pixels on a screen, or arcseconds all give the same date. No distance to the nebula is needed.
  • Distances between features work too. For two parcels, dij(t) = |vi − vj|(t − T), so separations obey the identical formula (handout part 2). Projection onto the sky also preserves the ratio.
  • Small motions amplify errors. r2 − r1 is a difference of two similar numbers. A 1 mm slip on a 4 mm movement is a 25% error in the age.

Step 1 · look before you measureBlink the two plates

Astronomers have discovered moving objects for a century by flipping rapidly between two photographs (a “blink comparator”; Pluto was found this way). Stars stay put; the nebula’s filaments visibly creep outward. Use the slider to cross-fade or press Blink.

What to notice
  • The 1999 photo has been registered to the 1956 one: it was shifted, rotated (−0.32°) and rescaled (+0.24%) so the six crosshair stars A1–C2 land on the same pixels (rms mismatch 0.6 px). Only the nebula moves now.
  • Outer features (1, 2, 4, 7, 11) slide noticeably outward; inner ones (6, 8, 10) barely move. Inner features will give the noisiest dates.
  • The 1999 plate shows sharper, finer filaments: a different telescope/emulsion. Some features look different in shape, so choose compact knots for your own features.

Step 4 · combineOne date from many

Your rows won’t agree. That is normal, and the spread tells you how trustworthy your answer is. Compare the averages for each type of measurement, as the handout suggests.

Why inner features misbehave

The uncertainty in the date grows like 43 · d₁₉₅₆ · σ / (Δd)². Feature 6 moves only about a pixel in 43 years, so a one-pixel click error can swing its date by thousands of years, or make it move “inward.” Rows with no outward motion can’t be used and are crossed out. The weighted mean gives each row a vote in proportion to 1/σ², so the large-motion outer features dominate automatically.

Step 5 · interpretWhy careful measurements don’t land on 1054

If you measure well, you will probably get a date in the 1100s, not 1054. You haven’t failed: professional astronomers get the same thing.

Trimble (1968) measured filament motions on plates spanning decades and found a convergence date near 1140. Nugent (1998) repeated it with modern plates and found about 1130 ± 16. A real explosion that happened in 1054 looks younger because the filaments are moving faster now than they did on average. Something has been pushing them.

That something is the pulsar. The spinning neutron star pours energy into a bubble of magnetic field and high-speed particles (the bluish synchrotron glow), and that bubble has been shoving the filaments outward, accelerating them for nearly a thousand years. Most supernova remnants slow down as they sweep up gas; the Crab is unusual in speeding up.

The sign of the error tells the story
If the debris has been…constant-speed date is…
coasting (constant speed)correct (1054)
slowing downtoo early (before 1054)
speeding up (Crab)too late (≈1130)

The method really computes τ* = r/v (current distance ÷ current speed). For constant acceleration a over the true age τ, r = v₀τ + ½aτ² and v = v₀ + aτ, so exactly τ − τ* = aτ²/(2v): positive (date too late) whenever a > 0.

The ≈80-year offset implies today’s filament speeds are about 9% above their average since 1054 (902 yr ÷ 826 yr to 1956).

Step 6 · explain it to a friendThe write-up

Your audience is a smart friend who has never taken astronomy. The handout asks for a several-page document with the photos, your table, an explanation of every calculation, and your results, all clearly labeled. A structure that works:

  1. The hook. The 1054 “guest star” and the question: can two photos tell us when it exploded?
  2. The photos. Both images with your own features (21+) marked, captioned with year and what the labels mean.
  3. The idea. Explain distance = speed × time with an everyday analogy (cars leaving an intersection, raisins in rising bread).
  4. The table. Every column headed with a quantity and its unit, e.g. Speed (mm/year). Distinguish pulsar→feature rows from feature↔feature rows.
  5. One worked example. Walk through a single row in plain words.
  6. The results. Your averages, the spread, which rows you trusted and why.
  7. The comparison. Your date vs. 1054, and why the Crab’s acceleration makes a later date expected.

For the instructorNotes, extensions, references

Teaching notes
  • Registration. The 1999 image in this page was aligned to 1956 by a least-squares affine fit to the centroids of the six reference stars (scale 1.0024, rotation −0.32°, shift ≈ 2.5 px; residuals 0.3–1.0 px). With the plates registered, the star-pair scale factor in Step 3 comes out ≈ 1.000. Asking “how do we know the photos line up?” is still a good systems-check question for non-majors, especially if they work from the unaligned printouts.
  • Expected student outcome. Outer features typically give 1000–1300; inner features scatter wildly. A class-wide median of 1100–1200 is a good result.
  • Class pooling. Have students paste rows into a shared sheet; the class distribution narrows visibly with N, a concrete intro to σ/√N.
  • Pulsar proper motion (~15 mas/yr) is negligible over 43 years at this image scale; mention only if asked.
Extension: velocities in km/s (for stronger students)

Convert the movement to an angular rate μ (arcsec/yr) using the plate scale (estimate it from the nebula’s known size, ≈ 7′ × 5′, or from the star pair separations with catalog coordinates), then vt [km/s] = 4.74 · μ[″/yr] · d[pc] with d ≈ 2000 pc. Compare with Doppler spectroscopy of the filaments (~1500 km/s); agreement between transverse and line-of-sight speeds is itself how the Crab’s distance is estimated (expansion parallax).

Extension: automate feature tracking (computational) 
# Sub-pixel feature shifts by phase cross-correlation (scikit-image)
import numpy as np
from skimage import io, color
from skimage.registration import phase_cross_correlation

a = color.rgb2gray(io.imread("CrabNeb_1956_8in.jpg"))
b = color.rgb2gray(io.imread("CrabNeb_1999_8in.jpg"))
pulsar = np.array([571, 586])                  # (row, col) in 1956 image

def shift_at(rc, half=24):
    r, c = rc
    pa = a[r-half:r+half, c-half:c+half]
    pb = b[r-half:r+half, c-half:c+half]
    s, err, _ = phase_cross_correlation(pa, pb, upsample_factor=20)
    return -s, err                              # displacement 1956 -> 1999

for rc in [(732, 204), (742, 345), (294, 456)]:  # features 1, 2, 4
    d, err = shift_at(rc)
    r1 = np.hypot(*(np.array(rc) - pulsar))
    r2 = np.hypot(*(np.array(rc) + d - pulsar))
    print(rc, d.round(2), round(1956 - 43*r1/(r2-r1)))

A grid of such patches gives a full velocity field; fitting v = (r − r₀)/τ by least squares recovers both the expansion center and the age, which makes a nice undergraduate computational project (compare the fitted center with the pulsar position). Note the different resolution and emulsion of the two plates, so smoothing the 1999 image to match 1956 before correlating helps.
