Calculating the age

The idea: a galaxy at distance d moving at speed v took time d ÷ v to get there. Since v = H₀ × d, that time is 1 ÷ H₀ for every galaxy. The only work is getting the units right.

  1. Write down your H₀ from the slider, in km/s per Mpc. The example below uses a made-up value, H₀ = 100 km/s/Mpc. Use your own value.
  2. Change megaparsecs to kilometers. One megaparsec is 3.086 × 10¹⁹ km, so H₀ = 100 km/s for every 3.086 × 10¹⁹ km.
  3. Find the time in seconds: divide the distance by the speed. Example: 3.086 × 10¹⁹ km ÷ 100 km/s = 3.086 × 10¹⁷ seconds.
  4. Change seconds to years. One year is 3.156 × 10⁷ seconds. Example: 3.086 × 10¹⁷ ÷ 3.156 × 10⁷ = 9.78 × 10⁹ years.
  5. Write it in billions of years. Example: 9.78 × 10⁹ years = 9.78 billion years. Now repeat steps 1 to 5 with your own H₀.

Check your arithmetic: steps 2 to 5 always combine into one number, so age in billions of years = 978 ÷ H₀. Example: 978 ÷ 100 = 9.78.

What you are doing

In 1929 Edwin Hubble noticed that the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us. If everything is flying apart at a steady rate, you can run the movie backward and ask: how long ago was everything in the same place?

speed = H₀ × distance

H₀ (“H-naught”) is the slope of the line. A galaxy 1 megaparsec away (about 3.26 million light-years) moves away at H₀ kilometers per second. The time since everything was together is distance ÷ speed, which is 1 ÷ H₀. With these units, that works out to 978 ÷ H₀ billion years.

Questions to answer

  1. Which set of galaxies gave the more consistent line, the nearby ones or the distant ones? Look at how much the dots wander from the line.
  2. The galaxy NGC 3031 (also called M81) is close to us but barely moving away. Galaxies also move around inside their groups, like people milling around in a moving train. Why does that random motion matter more for nearby galaxies than for distant ones?
  3. Your classmates had different samples. Why did everyone get slightly different ages? What does that tell you about scientific uncertainty?
  4. The oldest stars we know of are roughly 12 to 13 billion years old. Is your age for the universe consistent with that? What would it mean if it were not?
  5. Hubble’s own 1929 slope was about 500 km/s per Mpc. What age does that give? Why might that have bothered geologists, who already knew Earth was about 4.5 billion years old?
